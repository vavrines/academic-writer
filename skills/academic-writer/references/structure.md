# Paper Structure: What Each Section Must Do

Default model: IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion), the
standard for STEM papers. Adapt names to the venue, but the *jobs* stay.

## Title

- Specific enough to distinguish the paper from its neighbors; no "A Study of".
- Searchable: contain the terms a reader would actually search for.
- No abbreviations except universally known ones in the field.

## Abstract (150–250 words typical; check venue limit)

One sentence each, roughly in order:

1. **Context**: the problem area.
2. **Gap**: what is missing/unsolved.
3. **What we did**: the specific contribution.
4. **How**: method/approach in a phrase.
5. **Key result**: the strongest quantitative finding, with numbers if allowed.
6. **Implication**: why it matters.

No citations, no undefined abbreviations, no "results are discussed" filler.
The abstract must be true of the finished paper — write/rewrite it last.

## Introduction (typically 3–5 paragraphs, inverted funnel)

1. Paragraph 1: the problem and why it matters (broad).
2. Middle: what has been done — a *critical* synthesis, not a list. Group
   related work by approach and state what each group leaves open.
3. The gap sentence: "However, X remains unaddressed because…"
4. The contribution paragraph: "In this work, we …" — enumerate contributions
   (often as an explicit list) so a reviewer can quote it.
5. Optional: roadmap paragraph ("The rest of this paper is organized…") —
   include only if the venue's culture expects it.

Common faults: claiming novelty by ignorance (reviewers know the literature),
overselling, introducing notation/results early, burying the contribution on
page 2.

## Methods / Approach

- Completeness test: a competent researcher in the field could reproduce the
  work from this section plus cited references.
- Report choices, not just final settings: parameters, thresholds, datasets,
  software versions, hardware where it matters.
- Establish all notation here (or in a notation table) and keep it consistent
  to the end of the paper.
- For theoretical work: state assumptions before theorems; proofs or pointers
  to appendices.

## Results

- Organize by question/claim, not chronologically ("first we ran…").
- Every figure/table: introduced in the text before it appears, described
  (what it shows), and interpreted (what it means). Never "Figure 3 shows the
  results." — say which result and what to look at.
- Report negative or unexpected results honestly; hiding them is both
  unethical and a credibility risk.
- Numbers: sensible significant figures, units, uncertainty where relevant.

## Discussion / Conclusion

- Answer: what do the results mean, for whom, within what limits.
- Compare with prior work explicitly (agreements and contradictions).
- Limitations: name them before reviewers do. Scope conclusions to the
  evidence — "suggests", not "proves", unless it is a theorem.
- Future work: one short paragraph maximum; it is not a grant proposal.
- Do not introduce new results here.

## Related Work (when a separate section exists)

- Distinguish from the intro's critical synthesis: intro motivates the gap,
  this section positions technically.
- Cite generously and fairly. A reviewer who finds their relevant work
  uncited becomes a hostile reviewer.

## Acknowledgments / Funding / Conflicts

Follow venue rules exactly (some venues forbid identifying acknowledgments
during double-blind review).

## Appendices / Supplementary

Proofs, extra experiments, data tables. The main text must stand alone:
nothing essential to the core argument may live only in the appendix.
