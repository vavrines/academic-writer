# Academic English Style Guide

Rules below are defaults for STEM venues; defer to the target journal's guide
where it conflicts.

## Voice and tense

- Active voice when the actor matters ("We discretize Eq. (4) using…");
  passive is fine when the actor is obvious or irrelevant ("Samples were
  annealed at 400 °C").
- Present tense for established facts and what the paper itself does:
  "Figure 2 shows…", "Equation (3) implies…".
- Past tense for what you did in the study: "We measured…", "The simulation
  converged after…".
- Present perfect for past research with present relevance: "Several schemes
  have been proposed…".

## Hedging (calibrate, don't maximize)

- Match claim strength to evidence. Ladder, strongest to weakest:
  demonstrates / shows → indicates → suggests → is consistent with → may.
- Overclaiming gets papers rejected; over-hedging makes them unreadable.
  One well-placed "suggests" beats three hedges in one sentence.
- Ban empty intensifiers: "very", "extremely", "really", "quite".

## Concision

- Prefer: "because" over "due to the fact that"; "use" over "utilize";
  "to" over "in order to".
- Delete filler openings: "It is worth noting that", "It should be mentioned
  that", "As is well known".
- One term per concept, used consistently. Elegant variation ("the scheme" /
  "the approach" / "our method" for the same thing) confuses readers into
  thinking you mean different objects.
- Numbers as numerals in technical text (3 schemes, 10 ms), spelled out only
  at sentence start or by venue rule.

## Precision

- "This/that/these" must point to an identifiable noun. If the antecedent is
  a whole clause, rewrite: "This convergence failure…" not bare "This…".
- Define every abbreviation at first use (abstract and main text separately);
  delete abbreviations used fewer than ~3 times.
- Distinguish: "affect/effect", "compose/comprise", "which" (non-restrictive,
  with comma) vs. "that" (restrictive, no comma), "fewer" (countable) vs.
  "less" (mass).

## Common ESL patterns (frequent in Chinese authors' English)

- **Articles**: the hardest issue. Check every noun phrase for missing
  "the/a" — "the proposed method", "a finite volume scheme". When editing,
  fix these but note the pattern once rather than annotating every instance.
- **Overuse of "respectively"** and of "and so on / etc." in formal prose —
  replace with a complete list or "among others".
- **Long front-loaded modifiers**: "the above mentioned widely used high
  order finite volume method based on unstructured meshes" — break into a
  short noun phrase plus a clause.
- **"Obviously / It is obvious that"** — usually reads as condescending or as
  masking a gap; replace with the reason or delete.
- **Singular/plural with "data"** (plural in most venues), "criteria",
  "phenomena".
- **Sentence connectors**: "Besides," / "What's more," as sentence openers
  are informal — use "Moreover," / "In addition,". "On the contrary" is often
  misused where "In contrast" is meant.

## Paragraphs

- First sentence = topic sentence; a reader skimming first sentences should
  get the paper's argument.
- One idea per paragraph; ~3–8 sentences in most venues.
- Link paragraphs with logical connectives, not repetition.

## Inclusive and professional tone

- Criticize work, not authors: "Smith et al.'s model does not capture X"
  not "Smith et al. failed to…".
- Avoid gendered default pronouns; use "they" or rephrase.
